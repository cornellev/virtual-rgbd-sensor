\section{Modeling and Simulating a Virtual LiDAR}
The LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active sensor that provides accurate depth and reflection strength measurements
via continuous firing of laser pulses, which will be modeled as radiated waves. We consider the LiDAR sensor an active sounder whose
measurements are governed by wave propagation phenomena. The sensor modeled is the RoboSense RS-Helios, a 32-channel, 905\,nm pulsed
time-of-flight LiDAR. The resulting model is \emph{physics-based}: wave propagation sets the geometry (ray casting) and the radiometry
(the LiDAR range equation). On top of it sits a \emph{probabilistic} measurement model for timing noise and dropout.
The model is built in five stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item wave propagation and the ray approximation (Section~\ref{sec:wave});
    \item beam geometry and field of view (Section~\ref{sec:fov});
    \item line-of-sight visibility via ray casting (Section~\ref{sec:los});
    \item beam spreading, radiometry and echo detection (Sections~\ref{sec:beam}--\ref{sec:detect});
    \item the probabilistic measurement model (Section~\ref{sec:prob}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Wave Propagation and the Ray Approximation}\label{sec:wave}
The emitted laser pulse is modeled as a solution of the wave equation $\partial_t^2 u = \upsilon^2 \nabla^2 u$ with $\upsilon = c$, the speed
of light in air. We assume the targets lie in the emitter's far field, $r \gg$ the size of the emitting aperture, so the LiDAR can be treated
as a point source at the sensor origin $\vec{0}$ (each of its 32 channels is such a point emitter). The radial coordinate
$r = \|\vec{x}_T - \vec{0}\| = \sqrt{x_T^2 + y_T^2 + z_T^2}$ is the range from the sensor to a target at $\vec{x}_T \in \mathcal{W}$. For a wave
spreading from a point source, the wave equation in spherical coordinates has the outgoing solution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spherical}
    u(r,t) = \frac{1}{r} f_1(r - \upsilon t).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spherical} gives the three physical principles the rest of the model is built on.

\textbf{Time of flight.} The pulse shape $f_1$ travels outward unchanged at speed $\upsilon = c$. A pulse that reflects from a surface at range $r$
and returns to the sensor arrives after the round-trip time $\tau$, so the range is measured as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tof}
    \tau = \frac{2r}{c}, \qquad r = \frac{c\,\tau}{2}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Spherical spreading.} The amplitude decays as $1/r$, so the intensity, $I \propto u^2$, decays as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spreading}
    I(r) = I_0 \frac{r_0^2}{r^2},
\end{equation}
where $I_0$ is the intensity at reference distance $r_0$. Equivalently, the power crossing a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$ is conserved.
If the medium were bounded by parallel planes a distance $h$ apart, spreading would instead be cylindrical, with $I(r) = I_0\, r_0 / r$.
A LiDAR in open air has no such bounds, so the spherical law~\eqref{eq:spreading} applies to both the outgoing and the returning wave.

\textbf{Rays.} The 905\,nm wavelength is at least $10^{5}$ times smaller than any object in the scene. In this high-frequency (geometric optics) limit,
energy travels along rays normal to the wavefronts, which are straight lines in a homogeneous medium. This is what justifies simulating the
LiDAR by \emph{ray casting} instead of solving the wave equation over the whole scene.

\subsection{Beam Geometry and Field of View}\label{sec:fov}
The density of depth and intensity data that the LiDAR captures in one revolution is set by its hardware configuration.
The channels define the vertical angles $\theta$ at which beams are fired. The rpm defines how often the lasers fire per revolution,
which sets the horizontal (azimuth) angle resolution $\Delta\phi$. Channel $c \in \{1,\dots,32\}$ has an elevation $\theta_c$, a
horizontal calibration offset $\delta_c$, and a firing delay $\tau_c = 1.73\,(c-1)$\,$\mu$s within a block. These values are read from the
physical unit's DIFOP packets (\texttt{VERT\_CENTIDEG}, \texttt{HORIZ\_CENTIDEG}, \texttt{FIRING\_TSS\_US}).

A \emph{block} is one firing of all 32 channels and lasts $T_b = 55.56\,\mu$s. Successive blocks advance in azimuth by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dphi}
    \Delta\phi = 360^\circ \cdot \frac{\text{rpm}}{60} \cdot T_b,
\end{equation}
which gives $0.1^\circ$, $0.2^\circ$ or $0.4^\circ$ at 300, 600 or 1200\,rpm. Channel $c$ of block $k$ fires at
\begin{align}
    t_{k,c} &= k\,T_b + \tau_c, \label{eq:tfire}\\
    \phi^0_{k,c} &= k\,\Delta\phi + \Delta\phi\,\tau_c / T_b, \qquad \phi_{k,c} = \phi^0_{k,c} + \delta_c, \\
    \vec{o}_{k,c} &= (0, 0, h) + R_x\left(\cos\phi^0_{k,c},\, -\sin\phi^0_{k,c},\, 0\right), \\
    \vec{d}(\theta_c, \phi_{k,c}) &= \left(\cos\theta_c \cos\phi_{k,c},\; -\cos\theta_c \sin\phi_{k,c},\; \sin\theta_c\right). \label{eq:dir}
\end{align}
Here $h$ is the mount height and $R_x = 34.98$\,mm is the offset of the lens from the rotation axis. The sensor frame $\mathcal{F}_L$
is x forward, y left, z up, and azimuth increases clockwise. The field of view is therefore the set
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F} = \left\{ (r, \theta, \phi) : \theta \in \{\theta_1, \dots, \theta_{32}\} \subset [-15.98^\circ, +9.99^\circ],\;
    \phi \in [0^\circ, 360^\circ),\; r \in [r_{min}, r_{max}] \right\},
\end{equation}
with $[r_{min}, r_{max}] = [0.2, 150]$\,m. This gives a $360^\circ \times 26^\circ$ field of view with $360^\circ / \Delta\phi$ columns of 32 non-uniformly spaced rings: $0.5^\circ$ apart
near the horizon and up to $3^\circ$ apart at the edges. The output follows the Helios's MSOP/DIFOP UDP packet encoding.
Each MSOP packet holds 12 blocks, and each return is stored as a range quantized to 2.5\,mm and an 8-bit intensity.

\subsection{Line-of-Sight Visibility}\label{sec:los}
Each beam is cast as the ray $\vec{o} + t\vec{d}$, $t > 0$. The measured surface is the nearest intersection with any object $O_j$
in the scene. Each object moves with velocity $\vec{v}_j$ and is evaluated at the beam's own firing time~\eqref{eq:tfire}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:los}
    r^* = \min_j \left\{ t > 0 : \vec{o} - \vec{v}_j t_{k,c} + t\vec{d} \in \partial O_j \right\}, \qquad r^* \le r_{max}.
\end{equation}
Taking the minimum is what models occlusion: only the first surface along the line of sight is visible, and everything behind it is in shadow.
Because the channels fire at different times, moving targets show the same rolling-shutter skew as on the real sensor.
The intersection is solved analytically per primitive (ground plane, sphere, box slab test, or cylinder quadratic plus cap plane).
Each hit returns the surface normal $\vec{n}$ and reflectivity $\rho$, which give the angle of incidence $\cos\alpha = |\vec{n} \cdot \vec{d}|$.

\subsection{Beam Divergence: Spherical Spreading of the Footprint}\label{sec:beam}
A real laser beam is not an infinitely thin ray. It is a narrow cone of half-angle $\gamma$, so the \emph{footprint} it illuminates grows with range.
We model the transverse profile as Gaussian, with $1/e^2$ radius $w(r) = \gamma r$. The irradiance at transverse offset $\varrho$ from the beam axis is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gauss}
    E(\varrho, r) = \frac{2 P_t}{\pi w(r)^2} \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\varrho^2}{w(r)^2}\right) e^{-\sigma r}
                 = \frac{2 P_t}{\pi \gamma^2 r^2} \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\varrho^2}{\gamma^2 r^2}\right) e^{-\sigma r}.
\end{equation}
The peak irradiance falls as $1/r^2$: this is the spherical spreading law~\eqref{eq:spreading} applied to the outgoing beam, with a solid angle of $\pi\gamma^2$ instead of $4\pi$.

The footprint is sampled with $N$ sub-rays on concentric rings (1, 6, 12, \dots) out to the $1/e^2$ radius, each cast as in~\eqref{eq:los}.
Each sub-ray $i$ carries a fraction $w_i$ of the pulse energy, set by its Gaussian weight and footprint area and normalized so $\sum_i w_i = 1$.
The default is a full-angle divergence $2\gamma = 3$\,mrad (an assumed value typical of 905\,nm spinning LiDARs) and $N = 19$ sub-rays.
At 20\,m the footprint is then 6\,cm across. Setting $\gamma = 0$ recovers the ideal single-ray model.

\subsection{Radiometry: the LiDAR Range Equation}\label{sec:radiometry}
Sub-ray $i$ delivers power $w_i P_t e^{-\sigma r_i}$ to a surface patch at range $r_i$. A Lambertian surface of reflectivity $\rho$ re-radiates it
with radiant intensity $\rho\, w_i P_t e^{-\sigma r_i} \cos\alpha_i / \pi$ toward the sensor. That return is itself a point source,
and it spreads spherically~\eqref{eq:spreading} back to the receiver aperture $A_R$. The received power is the LiDAR range equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:range}
    P_i = \frac{P_t A_R}{\pi}\; w_i\, \frac{\rho_i \cos\alpha_i \, e^{-2\sigma r_i}}{r_i^2}
        \;\propto\; w_i\, \frac{\rho_i \cos\alpha_i \, e^{-2\sigma r_i}}{r_i^2}.
\end{equation}
Each term has a physical origin:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\rho\cos\alpha$: Lambertian reflection, which depends on the target's material and orientation;
    \item $1/r^2$: spherical spreading of the Lambertian return;
    \item $e^{-2\sigma r}$: two-way Beer--Lambert extinction by the atmosphere.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Extended and partial targets.} When a target fills the footprint, $\sum_i w_i = 1$ and~\eqref{eq:range} reduces to $P \propto \rho\cos\alpha\, e^{-2\sigma r}/r^2$.
A target narrower than the footprint instead intercepts a shrinking fraction $\sum w_i$ of the beam as range grows, so its return falls off faster
and a partly hit edge returns a weaker, range-blended echo.

\textbf{Koschmieder's law.} Fog, haze and aerosols scatter and absorb light along the path. Their extinction coefficient $\sigma$ is set from the
meteorological visibility $V$ by Koschmieder's law,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \frac{3.912}{V},
\end{equation}
with $\sigma = 0$ in clear air. Through $e^{-2\sigma r}$ in~\eqref{eq:range}, poor visibility shortens the detection range. Points become sparser and
fewer distant targets are seen. The effect is stronger for dark and oblique targets, whose $\rho\cos\alpha$ is already small.

\subsection{Echo Formation and Detection}\label{sec:detect}
The receiver records the returned pulse in time. It cannot separate contributions that arrive within one pulse width $\tau_p$ of each other,
which corresponds to the range resolution
\begin{equation}
    \Delta R = \frac{c\,\tau_p}{2} = 0.75\,\text{m} \quad (\tau_p = 5\,\text{ns}).
\end{equation}
Sub-ray contributions within $\Delta R$ of each other merge into one echo $e$, with total power $P_e = \sum_{i\in e} P_i$ and the power-weighted range
$r_e = \sum_{i\in e} P_i r_i / P_e$. This reproduces two real effects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{mixed pixels} at object edges, where foreground and background contributions blend;
    \item \emph{range walk} on surfaces hit at grazing angles, where the footprint itself spans a range interval.
\end{itemize}
An echo is detectable only if its power clears the receiver's noise threshold, $P_e \ge P_{min}$. The threshold is calibrated so that a target with
$\rho = 0.1$, at normal incidence and in clear air, is just detectable at $R_{10} = 110$\,m:
\begin{equation}
    P_{min} = \frac{0.1}{R_{10}^2}.
\end{equation}
For an extended target in clear air, this gives a closed-form maximum detection range that depends on the target's properties:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rdet}
    r_{det}(\rho, \alpha) = R_{10}\sqrt{10\,\rho\cos\alpha}.
\end{equation}
In fog, $r_{det}$ is the root of $\rho\cos\alpha\, e^{-2\sigma r}/r^2 = P_{min}$. Our unit is configured in \emph{strongest-return} mode,
so the strongest detectable echo is the one reported.

\subsection{Probabilistic Measurement Model}\label{sec:prob}
\textbf{Range noise.} The echo's time of flight~\eqref{eq:tof} is measured with Gaussian timing jitter, $\hat{\tau} = 2(r_e + b)/c + \sigma_t\,\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and the reported range is $\hat{r} = c\hat{\tau}/2$. Leading-edge timing jitter scales inversely with the signal-to-noise ratio,
and the SNR is proportional to $P_e$. The range standard deviation is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma}
    \sigma_r(P_e) = \frac{c\,\sigma_t}{2} = \sqrt{\sigma_0^2 + \left(\sigma_{th}\frac{P_{min}}{P_e}\right)^2},
\end{equation}
with a floor $\sigma_0 = 2$\,cm for strong returns and $\sigma_{th} = 5$\,cm of extra noise at the detection threshold.
Weak returns (far, dark, oblique or fogged targets) are thus noisier than strong ones. $\hat{r}$ is finally quantized to the protocol's 2.5\,mm,
and each return is independently dropped with probability $p_{drop}$.

\textbf{Intensity.} The reported intensity is range-compensated return power, which by~\eqref{eq:range} is the effective reflectivity:
\begin{equation}
    I = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(100\, P_e\, r_e^2,\; 1,\; 255\right) = 100\,\rho\cos\alpha\, e^{-2\sigma r}\textstyle\sum_{i\in e} w_i.
\end{equation}
This puts diffuse surfaces at 0--100 and retroreflectors ($\rho > 1$) above 100.

\textbf{Full measurement model.} The measurement for block $k$, channel $c$ is $z_{k,c} = (\hat{r}, I, \phi_{k,c}, \theta_c)$, produced by the
deterministic chain~\eqref{eq:dir}$\to$\eqref{eq:los}$\to$\eqref{eq:range}$\to$ echo selection plus the timing-jitter and drop-probability noise above.
Each sensor, environment and target variable enters $z$ through a specific term:
\begin{itemize}
    \item rpm sets the azimuth resolution~\eqref{eq:dphi}, and $h$ sets where the rings land on the ground, $\varrho_c = h/\tan|\theta_c|$;
    \item visibility enters through $\sigma$;
    \item reflectivity and incidence angle enter through $\rho\cos\alpha$, and target size relative to the footprint through $\sum w_i$;
    \item target motion enters through $t_{k,c}$ in~\eqref{eq:los}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Simulation Implementation and Verification}
The model is implemented in Rust (\texttt{lidar\_sim}). The ray casting, noise and packet-framing structure were adapted from ev-sim's LiDAR pipeline.
The beam footprint, echo formation and SNR-dependent timing models of Sections~\ref{sec:beam}--\ref{sec:prob} were added on top.
The simulator ray-traces a parking-lot scene containing ground ($\rho = 0.15$), a building, a wall, parked and moving cars (boxes),
pedestrians and poles (cylinders), a retroreflective sign ($\rho = 2.5$) and a 0.5\,m calibration sphere. It writes the MSOP/DIFOP UDP stream the real
Helios would send, as a \texttt{.pcap} file. That file is decoded by the same driver (\texttt{rslidar\_sdk\_node}) used for the physical sensor, so simulated
and real scans pass through an identical pipeline.

\section{Representation of Scans as a Range Graph}
A range graph is a 2D array representation of a LiDAR scan, specifically encoding vertical and horizontal angle along with cartesian $(x, y, z)$ information 
of each laser beam in an ordered structure that models the LiDAR's hardware configurations. Range graph elements are represented as RangeGraph$[\theta][\phi]$,
which contains 3D point of collision $(x, y, z)$, range $r$, and reflection intensity of a laser fired at vertical angle $\theta$ and horizontal angle $\phi$.

\section{Two-Layer-Graph Clustering}
Point cloud segmentation allows us to recreate obstacle regions and geometry through exploitation of LiDAR hardware configuration and the physical nature of
objects in 3D space. Obstacle regions can be used for tracking algorithms that better inform longer-horizon planning, along with point cloud compression and
cohesive visualization of the environment around the sensor platform. "Two-Layer-Graph Clustering for Real-Time 3D LiDAR Point Cloud Segmentation" (Yang, Wang,
Lin, et al.) constructs range graphs from prior knowledge of a LiDAR's beam firing pattern, then performs two separate segmentations on the range graph for
accurate, real-time clustering.

\textbf{First segmentation.} Clustering horizontally adjacent points to populate a set graph. This serves to significantly reduce the amount of points that need
to be traversed and explored in the second segmentation by first conducting a parallelizable clustering among points within the same channel or vertical angle.

\textbf{Second segmentation.} Merging vertically adjacent nodes via BFS or possible other graph traversal methods over a reduced set of set nodes. Given a SetNode,
we search for vertically adjacent nodes that intersect with the current horizontal segment, as defined by RangeGraph node's $(\theta_{start}, \theta_{end})$.

\section{KLT Tracking via Intensity-Depth Fusion}
\textbf{Proposal.} The idea here is to simulate a form of camera-LiDAR sensor fusion without actually requiring synchronization between a physical camera and LiDAR.
The RoboSense Helios LiDAR outputs UDP packets containing not only vertical $\theta$ and horizontal $\phi$ firing angles of each laser, the range $r$ travelled 
prior to collision, and associated timestamps $t$, but also the reflection strength of each laser, also known as intensity. Reflection strength is dependent on the
material of the surface that each laser beam collided with. As a result, we can essentially construct a sparse image of the environment around the sensor platform
via an intensity map. This intensity map can be passed in with associated depth information to the KLT algorithm to perform some pseudo-optical flow on a sequence 
of LiDAR scans.
"The intensity measured by the LiDAR can be decomposed into four main factors under the Lambertian reflectance assumption: measurement  distance, the surface reflectance,
the strength of the incident ray, and the incident angle" (Dai et al.) -- the same dependence as the range equation~\eqref{eq:range} derived in Section~\ref{sec:radiometry}.
At close range ($R < 12$\,m), lens defocusing additionally attenuates the reported intensity, which we would need to correct for before using it as a KLT feature.
